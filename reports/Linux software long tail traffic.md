# Mapping Linux's release-date long tail

**Short answer: the "X release date" opportunity is a thin head and a very long tail, and nobody could measure it directly.** We had no paid keyword-tool data (Ahrefs, Semrush, Keyword Planner), so every search-volume bucket below is an estimate built from measured stand-ins: Wikipedia pageviews, Flathub install counts and Debian popcon counts. On that basis, probably **zero Linux projects exceed 100k monthly searches for a release-date query, about 5 sit in 10k-100k, about 35 in 1k-10k, about 350 in 100-1k and about 2,000 in 10-100**, with thousands more below 10. The best first targets are projects whose next release has a fixed or already published date and whose search results look thin: Fedora 46, Linux kernel 7.4 to 7.8, Debian 14, Linux Mint 27, KDE Plasma, Ubuntu 27.04 and a set of Ubuntu/Fedora-based derivative distros. Roughly **100-150 pages** covers that first wave. Each project's release-date searches are small in absolute terms, so the plan only pays off if pages are templated and cheap to produce. Labels used throughout: **[SOURCED]** read directly from a named source, **[COMPUTED]** calculated by us from raw source data, **[ESTIMATED]** our inference or assumption.

## How big the Linux software universe is, and how steep its tail is

The universe is huge only if you count every package. The notable, searchable part is a few thousand projects. Flathub lists **3,395 apps** with public per-app install counters [SOURCED] ([Flathub API](https://flathub.org/api/v2/stats)). Computing from those counters, **78 apps (2.3%) have over 1M installs, 485 have 100k-1M, 1,475 have 10k-100k, 1,176 have 1k-10k and 181 have under 1k** [COMPUTED]. The median app has about 15k installs, and the top 100 apps hold about 59% of all installs [COMPUTED]. The Snap Store shows about **8,805 listings** [SOURCED, approximate: filters undocumented] ([Snap API](https://api.snapcraft.io/api/v1/snaps/search?size=1)), but publishes no public install counts, so its tail shape is unknown. The AppImage catalog lists **3,348 apps** with no usage data [SOURCED] ([feed](https://appimage.github.io/feed.json)). These three lists overlap heavily; we did not de-duplicate them, so do not add them together.

Package-level counts show how misleading raw totals are. Debian's popularity-contest tracks **226,478 package names**, but only **1,279 have 100k+ installs, about 6,000 have 10k+, and 141,854 have under 10 installs** [COMPUTED] ([popcon](https://popcon.debian.org/all-popcon-results.txt.gz)). Repology tracks **335,425 projects** [SOURCED] ([Repology](https://repology.org/repositories/statistics)), but we could not compute how many appear in many repositories, so its popular segment is unmeasured. Distros are a stable small set: **958 listed, about 274 active** in early 2024 [SOURCED] ([Linux Magazine](https://www.linux-magazine.com/Issues/2024/280/Digging-into-DistroWatch)), flat at roughly 275-285 for a decade [ESTIMATED from sourced points]. Desktop and launcher projects are a long tail of hobby repos on GitHub (for example 1,975 window-manager topic repositories) topped by a few stars like Hyprland at 39,809 [SOURCED] ([GitHub](https://api.github.com/repos/hyprwm/Hyprland)); GitHub topic tags are self-applied, so these counts overstate maintained projects.

## Search demand: a proxy ladder, then an estimated bucket table

The only direct demand evidence is Wikipedia. Across **130 Linux/FOSS English articles** (12-month mean, Oct 2025 to Sep 2026), **0 had under 1k monthly views, 69 had 1k-10k, 58 had 10k-100k and 3 had 100k+** (Telegram, Nginx, Android, none Linux-specific); the median was about 8.6k [COMPUTED from the [Wikimedia API](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/)]. This sample is biased toward notable projects, so the true tail below 1k is missing. The "version history" pages are the closest match to release-date intent: Ubuntu about **18k-35k a month**, Linux kernel **20k-31k**, Firefox **6.7k-10.8k**, Fedora **4.6k-9.3k** (peaking each April and October), and Linux Mint only **170-380** [SOURCED, measured]. That 100x gap between Ubuntu and Mint shows how fast release-intent demand falls off outside the top few. Version-history views were between about 1% and 65% of each project's main article views (Mint 0.8%, Ubuntu 65%) [COMPUTED], so the conversion from brand interest to release interest is not stable.

Assuming release-date searches run at roughly 1-10% of a project's Wikipedia brand views, with the high end for fixed-cadence distros (our assumption, not sourced), we get the table below. Treat each count as a range of about plus or minus 50%, and treat the bucket edges as soft.

| Monthly searches for the project's release-date query family | Estimated projects | What lives here (examples) | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100k+ | 0 | Nothing Linux-specific; Ubuntu and Firefox are the closest | ESTIMATED |
| 10k-100k | about 5 | Ubuntu, Linux kernel, Fedora, Debian, Firefox | ESTIMATED |
| 1k-10k | about 35 | Mint, Arch, GNOME, KDE, Blender, LibreOffice, Steam/SteamOS, Kali, openSUSE, Pop!_OS, big languages | ESTIMATED |
| 100-1k | about 350 | Mid-size distros and DEs, most Flathub apps above 100k installs (563 of them [COMPUTED]) | ESTIMATED |
| 10-100 | about 2,000 | Most Flathub apps with 10k-100k installs (1,475 [COMPUTED]), small distros, WMs | ESTIMATED |
| Under 10 | the remaining thousands (about 1,300 Flathub apps, most of the 8,805 snaps, nearly all of the 226k popcon names) | Not worth individual pages | ESTIMATED |

The anchors that justify the shape are measured: 11 Wikipedia pages at 25k+ views, about 30 at 10k-25k and 69 at 1k-10k [COMPUTED]. The same logic cross-checks against general keyword data: Ahrefs reports that about 93% of its 2.3 billion US keywords get under 10 searches a month and only 17,730 exceed 100k ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/long-tail-keywords/)) [SOURCED]. Demand is also lumpy by time. Ubuntu and Fedora pages spike every April and October, the SteamOS article ran about 3x baseline in November 2025, and COSMIC and Hyprland show one-off peaks [SOURCED, measured]. A release-date page earns most of its yearly traffic in a few weeks, so it must be live well before the window.

## Predictable cadence plus thin results marks the best targets

Predictability is the filter that matters, and our evidence covers only part of the field. Confirmed dates exist for **Ubuntu 27.04 (22 April 2027)** ([OMG Ubuntu](https://www.omgubuntu.co.uk/2026/10/ubuntu-2704-opens-for-development)), **GNOME 52 (17 March 2027)** ([GNOME calendar](https://release.gnome.org/calendar/)) and the kernel's **9-10 week cadence** after 7.3 on 18 October 2026 ([endoflife.date](https://endoflife.date/linux)) [all SOURCED]. From that cadence we derive Linux 7.4 around 20-27 December 2026, with holiday slippage likely [COMPUTED/ESTIMATED]. Fedora 46 has no official date found, though spring 2027 is the usual window, and Fedora 44 and 45 both slipped through Go/No-Go votes ([Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/Fedora-45-First-No-Go)) [SOURCED; Fedora 45's actual ship date is unresolved]. Debian 14 "forky" is expected around mid-2027 on a volunteer estimate, not an official date ([Linuxiac](https://linuxiac.com/debian-13-freeze-begins-in-march-debian-15-codename-revealed/)) [UNVERIFIED]. Linux Mint's next release, renamed from 23 to **27 "Adrien"**, targets December 2026 ([It's FOSS](https://itsfoss.com/news/linux-mint-27-announced.md)) [SOURCED, exact date unverified]. The rename leaves stale "Mint 23" pages and fragmented queries, which is an opening.

On competition, our web searches found thin or outdated coverage for Fedora 46, Linux 7.4, Debian 14 and Mint 23/27 (results were news items, forum reposts and stale aggregator pages), while Ubuntu LTS, Python, Node, PHP, Firefox and Kubernetes likely have strong official and Wikipedia results [ESTIMATED; this was a web search, not a Google results check, so rankings are unverified]. The incumbent tracker endoflife.date lists released versions and end-of-life dates but did not yet list Ubuntu 27.04, and it offers no milestones, slip history or countdowns ([endoflife.date](https://endoflife.date/ubuntu)) [SOURCED]. That gap, forward-looking pages with beta and RC dates and "delayed again" updates, is the product. Rough category tallies from the project list: about 40-60 projects have some predictable cadence, of which about 15-20 have fixed published schedules (Ubuntu, Fedora, GNOME, kernel, Firefox, Rust, Python, Node, PHP, Go, Kubernetes, LibreOffice, Postgres, Kali, Tails) [ESTIMATED, unsourced]. Derivatives that inherit Ubuntu or Fedora timing (Zorin, Pop!_OS, Nobara, Bazzite, elementary, CachyOS) have predictable bases but lagged, uncertain own dates, so they likely face the weakest competition [ESTIMATED].

## What to build first, and how many pages

Start with the 20-30 projects where a firm or derivable date meets thin search results, then expand to derivatives. The sequence is below, with page counts assuming two or three pages per project (next release, the one after, and a "delay tracker" or schedule page where slips are common).

| Priority | Targets | Pages | Why | Label |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fedora 46/47, Linux 7.4 to 7.8, Debian 14/15, Mint 27/28, Ubuntu 27.04/27.10/28.04, GNOME 52/53, KDE Plasma next two | about 30-40 | Biggest demand tier (10k-100k and 1k-10k), dates known or derivable, thin results; publish before the April 2027 and October 2027 spikes | ESTIMATED |
| 2 | Derivatives and mid-size distros: Zorin, Pop!_OS/COSMIC, elementary, Nobara, Bazzite, CachyOS, Manjaro, EndeavourOS, openSUSE, MX, Kali, Tails | about 30-40 | 100-1k tier, inherited base dates, likely weakest competition | ESTIMATED |
| 3 | Apps and tools with regular trains: LibreOffice, Blender, Thunderbird, Wine, Mesa, Godot, Rust, Go, Python, Node, PHP, Postgres, Kubernetes | about 30-40 | Mostly 100-1k, but cadence is clear; Python, Node, PHP and Kubernetes need a quick check of competing official pages first | ESTIMATED |

That totals about **100-150 pages for the first wave**. The roughly 2,000 projects in the 10-100 tier are not worth individual hand-built pages; reach them only through an automated template fed by endoflife.date-style data, and only after wave one shows ranking traction. Combined release-date demand for wave one is plausibly **30k-100k searches a month in peak months**, and capturing 10-20% would mean roughly 3k-20k visits a month at peaks [ESTIMATED, unvalidated assumption]. Plan for that range being wrong by 2x either way.

## What we could not measure

Several limits bound every number above. There is no paid keyword-tool data, so no query-level volumes exist and the bucket table is a model, not a measurement; the stated 1-10% release-intent conversion was assumed, and our measured Wikipedia ratios ranged from 0.8% to 65%. Release schedules were verified for only about a third of the projects on the target list; not checked at all were Fedora 46's official date, KDE Plasma's forward schedule, Firefox's cadence (reports of a two-week cycle conflict with a four-week one), and about 30 others including LibreOffice, Blender, Kali, Python and Kubernetes. No live Google results were captured, so the "weak competition" calls are hypotheses from web searches. DistroWatch blocked automated access (HTTP 403), so no distro popularity ranking was obtained; Repology's many-repository distribution could not be computed because its API responses were too large; and Snap and AppImage have no usage data. The Wikipedia sample is English-only and skewed toward notable projects, a Debian version-history drop from about 22k-35k to about 1.9k a month is probably a page move rather than lost demand (cause unverified), and Fedora 45's actual release date (27 October versus 3 November) is unresolved.

## Conclusion

The central lesson is that traffic and opportunity point in different directions. The biggest single queries (Ubuntu, kernel, Firefox) are also the most contested and are served by official pages, while the rest of the market drops about 100x by the time you reach Mint-sized projects. The edge for a new site is therefore forward-looking, regularly updated schedule pages (slips, betas, countdowns, renamed releases) on mid-sized projects where existing pages are stale, not coverage of the head.

Before committing to the full 150 pages, the cheapest next step is to validate the model: pull Ahrefs or Keyword Planner volumes for about 15 priority queries (Fedora 46, Linux 7.4, Debian 14, Mint 27, Plasma 6.9 and similar), run live Google checks on those same queries, and complete the missing schedules from endoflife.date. If measured volumes land within a factor of two of the bucket table, proceed to wave one; if not, rescale the page plan from the real numbers.
